### Self-play temperature diagnosis

A read-only analysis of the 3,490 archived checkpoint-30–40 self-play games
covered 252,148 positions. All selected-move probabilities matched the
independently stored selected-child visit counts.

The current move rule samples in proportion to root visit counts (`tau=1`) at
every move. Across the corpus:

- 53.30% of selected moves were below the visit-count argmax;
- 30.64% had less than half the argmax move's visit share;
- 14.52% had less than one quarter of the argmax move's visit share;
- mean visit-share regret (argmax share minus selected share) was 0.1087.

The observed below-argmax rate closely matched the theoretical `tau=1`
expectation of 53.29%, confirming that this is the intended sampling rule and
not a decoding artifact. A counterfactual `tau=1` before move 12 and `tau=0`
thereafter reduces the expected below-argmax rate to 5.42% and mean regret to
0.0208. Using late `tau=0.25` instead gives 26.41% and 0.0341; late `tau=0.5`
gives 39.58% and 0.0583.

The archive does **not** establish that temperature causes late blunders.
Moves 48–63 were below argmax 66.34% of the time, but the visit distributions
were relatively flat and mean regret was only 0.0975. From move 64 onward the
rates fell to 56.67% below argmax, 19.48% below half, 4.36% below one quarter,
and 0.0775 mean regret. More importantly, winners sampled below argmax more
often than losers in both late phases: 68.74% versus 64.38% for moves 48–63,
and 64.21% versus 48.37% from move 64 onward. This is observational and
position-correlated, but it gives no support to the specific late-blunder
hypothesis.

The next diagnostic, if desired, is a deeper deterministic re-search of
archived positions and selected/argmax alternatives. That also requires no
training and can be run without uploading examples. The reusable archive
analysis is `scripts/analyze_selfplay_temperature.py`.

### Separated-tail analysis

A second read-only analysis replayed all 3,490 checkpoint-30–40 archives
(254,055 complete-game positions, including boundary-checkpoint examples).
It waits until both players have used all flags, conservatively enumerates
future causal propagation paths for each player, and declares separation only
when the two reachable-cell sets are disjoint. It then attempts to maximize
each player's independent remaining score with a memoized exact solver.

Separation was found in 3,229 games (92.52%). In 2,699 of those games both
players still had reachable cells; in 530 the opponent had none. The first
separated move had median 61, mean 60.7, and interquartile range 55–66. The
remaining recorded tail had median 11 and mean 13.39 examples.

If every separated tail could be resolved, the corpus would have omitted
43,226 examples (17.01%) and 188,681 seconds (52.4 hours, 11.51%) of recorded
MCTS search. With the analysis solver's deliberately small per-player limits
of 50,000 states and 0.5 seconds, 3,003 tails completed. Those completed tails
account for 40,611 examples (15.99%) and 176,917 seconds (49.1 hours, 10.79%)
of recorded search. Median solve time was 0.40 ms per attempted player, p95 was
170 ms, and 226 difficult games hit the cap and would have fallen back to
normal self-play.

Validation results were clean:

- all 2,827 separated positions with a following archived position reproduced
  the production C++ mover state and score gain exactly;
- none of the 3,229 games regained overlapping reachability later;
- the tight reachability enumeration never hit its safe relaxed fallback;
- all 3,003 reconstructed final scores reproduced the stored winner;
- neither player's archived final score exceeded the exact solver's maximum.

The exact optimal-tail winner matched the stochastic archived winner in
2,942/3,003 solved games (97.97%). The 61 differences are explained by a
player leaving locally available points on the table; they are concentrated
at small optimal score margins. Agreement was 2,871/2,890 (99.34%) at margin
at least 2, 2,689/2,692 (99.89%) at margin at least 5, and 2,359/2,359
(100%) at margin at least 10. A conservative 10-point gate would still omit
24,744 examples (9.74%) and 96,790 seconds (26.9 hours, 5.91%) of recorded
search in this corpus.

A corrected rerun quantified a possible 3-point gate. With small wall-clock
variance in which Python solves hit the cap, margin 3 covered 2,824 games,
omitted 35,960 examples and 153,301 recorded search seconds; margin 5 covered
2,698 games, 32,849 examples and 137,690 seconds. Thus 5→3 would have added
126 games, 3,111 omitted examples and 15,612 seconds (4.3 hours) of saved
search in this corpus. Optimal-vs-stochastic winner agreement was 99.54% at
margin 3 versus 99.89% at margin 5; those differences measure suboptimal
stochastic continuation, not solver correctness. A 3-point gate is a
reasonable next setting, although checkpoints 51–60 retained 5 for a clean
comparison with the preceding round.

The C++ port was deployed in active mode for the checkpoint-41–60
data-producing rounds. It used the chosen 5-point gate, 50,000 states and 50
ms per player, and falls back to ordinary MCTS on overlap or any solve limit.
Shadow mode remains available as a small validation aid.

A dry-run checkpoint-40 validation produced 80 complete games at 200 MCTS runs
per move. Of those, 47 reached a qualifying shadow decision and all 47
predicted winners matched the normally played result. Qualifying solves ranged
from tens of microseconds to roughly 42 ms. An earlier production-shaped wave
also found qualifying solves of 5.3 ms and 34.5 ms, but its continuations hit
the deliberately configured game deadline before comparison. Both runs were
dry runs and wrote no training examples.

The C++ implementation and its design documentation are in
`cpp/flagz_tail.{h,cc}`, `cpp/flagz_tail.md`, and
`cpp/flagz_tail_test.cc`. `NeuralMCTS::PlayGame` invokes it before MCTS search.
The controls are carried through `TrainingParameters` and the corresponding
`HEXZ_TAIL_SOLVER_*` environment variables.

Commit `ec1f285` also integrates the resolver into the remote C++ CPU player.
It returns a score-maximizing move before entering neural MCTS when the same
bounded separation proof succeeds. The rebuilt player image was used on both
sides of the checkpoint-50 versus checkpoint-40 match. Its two halves took
365.5 and 362.0 seconds, versus 423.0 and 452.7 seconds for the previous
checkpoint-40 versus checkpoint-30 match. That 17% wall-time reduction is
consistent with the intended endgame speedup, although it is not a controlled
same-model timing comparison.

The reusable implementation is split between

## Start here next

The checkpoint-51–60 round failed its endpoint test: checkpoint 60 is
significantly weaker than checkpoint 50 in the paired arena. Do **not**
continue the unchanged self-play/training regime merely because checkpoint 60
is latest. Checkpoint 50 remains the strongest demonstrated model and should
be the comparison or deployment choice.

Reasonable next options are:

- investigate training/search changes before generating another 250k rows;
- benchmark checkpoint 50 against checkpoint 20 or 0 to quantify cumulative
  strength with a wider signal;
- arena-screen checkpoint 52 only if locating a transient within-round peak is
  useful; its offline metrics were best in the failed round, but no playing-
  strength claim exists for it;
- lower the tail-solver gate from 5 to 3 when self-play resumes, since the
  corrected archive analysis supports that efficiency tradeoff.

Preserve the replay window and optimizer state, and do not initialize a new
candidate or overwrite existing checkpoints.
